What this prototype tests

Language models can now answer open questions about personal health data, but they can state numbers fluently and wrongly, and long answers don't suit a smartwatch or a quick voice exchange. Wristside separates the two jobs.

  1. Reading the question is separate from the numbers. A language model (Claude, in the full version) or built-in rules turn the question into a query plan: metric, dates, comparison. Neither ever writes a number.
  2. The page computes every number from the daily records, so each figure can be traced back to the days behind it.
  3. Answers come in layers: the answer, then a comparison, then context. Switch the structure above to see what a glance, a voice reply or a phone screen might carry.
  4. Data quality is part of the answer. Days with no data or low wear time are named and, where they would distort an average, left out.

Study mode

Run a study session (top of the page) shows six answers in one of three structures, counterbalanced across participants, and records accuracy, time, confidence and trust. Results stay in the browser until you download them as a CSV.

Next steps

Compare free-form model answers with these grounded, layered answers on accuracy, time to understand and trust, first in a lab study and then in a short diary study with people's own data. A further question: can people tell when an answer is wrong?

The data here is generated for demonstration and describes no real person. This prototype does not give medical advice. If Claude is unavailable in your view, questions are read by built-in rules and the answers are computed the same way.
